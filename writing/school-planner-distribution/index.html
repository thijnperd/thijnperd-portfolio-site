A school planner is a distribution problem

The calendar is the part you see. What made W3Plan usable was the school's data, an installer that updates itself, and a boundary that keeps student schedules on the device.

Published
27 Nov 2026
Reading
4 minutes
Topics
Desktop, distribution
Project
W3Plan

The calendar is the easy part

A planner app assumes you already know what has to be done. At school, the hardest question comes earlier: which tests are coming, which subjects they belong to, and how many study hours each one deserves. The school publishes a test calendar, the study hours are supposed to be divided across subjects, and the two live in different places. Most students resolve this with a paper agenda and a screenshot.

W3Plan starts from the school's PTA export instead. Exams, domain hours, per-test checklists and grade calculators all sit on one calendar, because the calendar is already the shape the week has. The interface is the visible part. It is not the part that took the most care.

Import is the product

The current import covers 643 PTA components across 29 subjects. 291 of those carry a date; a separate SE overview adds 133 test moments. That is too much to enter by hand and small enough to ship as a read-only dataset. The app loads it from Supabase or from a local JSON file, depending on where it is running.

This is the difference between a demo week and a real one. Once the school's exams are on the calendar, a study block has something to divide. Without the import, the planner is a nicer-looking blank page; with it, the first screen already knows the week.

The privacy boundary simplifies the architecture

PTA data is published by the school, so it is treated as public and read-only. A student's own schedule is not. It stays on the device, and there are no student accounts. That one decision removes the login flow, the session expiry, the password reset and the reason to keep a copy of anyone's week on a server.

It also removes a feature: there is no account sync between machines, so the schedule lives with the device it was made on. That is a real cost, but it is the kind of cost that can be explained in a sentence. A planner for a school week does not need a multi-tenant backend to be useful.

Distribution is the feature

The desktop builds auto-update. They ship as a Windows installer and as a DMG or ZIP on macOS; the ZIP is what the updater uses. Installing and updating turn out to be the least glamorous and most fragile part of the app: two platforms, two sets of failure modes, and no way to fix a bad release by editing a server.

A web build avoids installers, but it does not remove the data boundary: the schedule still has to stay on the device rather than becoming a server session. The desktop build is the version that reaches a classroom laptop and keeps its files there. The cost is that distribution becomes the product.

Eight students is a real test

W3Plan was built for senior secondary students at Were Di Drie. It has been adopted by 8+ final-year students and praised by teachers. That is not a mass market, but it is enough to test the parts a sample week never does: a real PTA export, a real exam period, and a real student deciding whether to trust the plan.

The project is a team effort. Billie, Yannis Raijmakers and I built the initial base app together, and Yannis and I kept developing it out for the school. The division mattered less than the shared constraint: the data model had to survive contact with a real school calendar.

What is still missing

There is no mobile app, no account sync, and the import is shaped by one school's data. Those are the same boundaries as the local-first choice, seen from the other side. Adding another school would mean mapping another export, not adding another view to the calendar.

That is the lesson worth keeping: for a tool built around an institution, start with the data and the install. The calendar is the last thing to polish, not the first. W3Plan is at its best when the school's data is already there and the student only has to decide what to do with the week.
